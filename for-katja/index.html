chapter 1 · the puzzle

A plane is really heavy

A normal holiday plane weighs about 70,000 kg when it takes off. That's around 12 elephants. The really big ones weigh as much as 60 elephants.

Heavy things fall. So what is holding all those elephants up? The answer is something invisible that's all around you right now: air.

chapter 2 · air is real stuff

Your hand out of a car window

You've probably done this: in a moving car, hold your hand flat out of the window. Then tilt the front of your hand up a little. Whoosh, the air lifts your hand up!

Air feels like nothing, but when you move through it fast, it pushes back like water. A plane's wing is basically a giant, very clever hand.

chapter 3 · the wing's trick

Push air down, get pushed up

This is like a wind tunnel with little smoke lines. A wing is curved on top and tilted a tiny bit. As the plane moves, the wing bends the air downwards. Watch the lines behind it.

When you push something one way, it pushes you back the other way. Like swimming: you push water backwards and you move forwards. The wing pushes tonnes of air down every second, so the air pushes the wing up. That push is called lift.

chapter 4 · speed is everything

Why planes race down the runway

The wing only pushes enough air down when it moves really fast. Slowly, it lifts almost nothing. That's why the plane zooms along the runway first.

Around 250 km/h (more than twice as fast as a car on the Autobahn) the wings make enough lift to carry all 12 elephants. The pilot gently lifts the nose, and up we go.

chapter 5 · the engines

A giant fan that throws air backwards

So who makes the plane go fast? The engines. Each one is a huge fan. It grabs air at the front and throws it out the back super fast.

Throwing air backwards pushes the plane forwards. Same as letting go of a blown-up balloon: the air rushes out one way, the balloon zooms the other way.

chapter 7 · steering

Little flaps do the steering

There's no steering wheel on the outside. Instead, the wings and tail have small flaps that move up and down. Each flap changes how the air is pushed there, a bit like steering a boat with a paddle.

Tap a button and watch the pink and yellow flaps.
chapter 8 · the scary question

What if the engines stop?

The plane does not fall like a stone. Remember, the wings make lift as long as the plane moves forward. With no engines, the pilot just points the nose a little bit down and the plane glides, like a paper plane.

From cruising height (about 10 km up), a passenger jet can glide roughly 150 km. That's about Freiburg to Stuttgart, plenty of time to find an airport. And planes have two or more engines, so losing all of them is extremely rare.

chapter 9 · the wobbly bits

Turbulence is just waves in the air

Air isn't perfectly still. It has currents and bumps, like water has waves. When the plane flies through them it wobbles, like a boat on a lake or a car on a bumpy road.

It feels weird, but it's a comfort problem, not a danger problem. Wings are built to bend a lot: in tests, big planes' wingtips flex upwards by several metres without breaking. Just keep your seatbelt on (and hold your tea).

chapter 10 · little questions

Things you might be wondering

Why do my ears pop?

Inside the plane, the air is a bit thinner than on the ground, like being on top of a 2,000 m mountain. Your ears have a tiny pocket of air that needs to catch up. Swallowing, yawning or chewing gum lets it even out. Pop!

Why do planes fly so high?

Up at 10 km the air is thin, so it pushes back much less. Less push-back means the plane goes faster and uses less fuel. It's also above most clouds and storms.

How fast does it go up there?

About 850 km/h. Berlin in about one hour from here. It doesn't feel fast because there's nothing close by to zoom past.

Why is it −50 °C outside?

The higher you go, the colder the air gets (that's why mountain tops have snow). At cruising height it's around −50 °C. The cabin is heated, so you're cosy inside.

Why are the windows round, not square?

The cabin is pumped full of air, a bit like a balloon. Sharp corners collect all the stress in one spot and could crack. Round corners spread it out evenly. Engineers learned this the hard way in the 1950s.

Why airplane mode?

A phone searching for signal can make a faint buzzing in the pilots' headsets, like when a phone is near a speaker. It won't crash a plane, it's just annoying for the pilots and keeps the radio clear.

What are the white lines behind planes?

Engines breathe out hot, damp air. Up where it's −50 °C, that water instantly freezes into tiny ice crystals. Same as your breath making a cloud on a cold winter morning.

Why do they dim the lights when landing?

So your eyes are already used to the dark. If everyone ever needed to get out quickly at night, they could see straight away.

Is flying actually safe?

Yes. Per kilometre travelled, flying is far safer than going by car. The drive to the airport is statistically the riskiest part of the trip.
